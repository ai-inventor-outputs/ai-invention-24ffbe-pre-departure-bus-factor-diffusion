## Research Findings

PART A — OSS-native positioning for a pre-departure authority-diffusion construct.

(A1) CHAOSS's Contributor Absence Factor (formerly Bus Factor) is defined by sorting contributors descending by contribution volume and counting how many are needed to reach 50% of total contributions [1]. CHAOSS's own knowledge base explicitly says this metric 'can be measured both ways' — as a single-point-in-time snapshot, or longitudinally by measuring at regular intervals to observe change over time [1]. The sibling Elephant Factor (organizational diversity: minimum companies needed to reach 50% of commits) is explicitly documented as a snapshot-only metric, with CHAOSS itself warning that computing it over the life of a product 'may misrepresent the current level of organizational diversity' [2]. Neither metric ships a defined trend/trajectory shape or a pre-departure predictive framing; CHAOSS names the longitudinal option for Contributor Absence Factor but does not formalize or validate it as predictive of anything. Adjacent metrics — Newcomer Experience (onboarding quality), Conversion Rate (return-contribution rate), Occasional Contributors (engagement breadth) [3] — do not track authority dispersing away from a founder either. This hypothesis's diffusion-trajectory construct is therefore best framed as operationalizing and validating the longitudinal variant CHAOSS gestures at but does not define, not as an unprecedented concept.

(A2) Apache's own graduation guide operationalizes 'diversity' as a binary, committee-judged gate: a project is 'diverse' when 'not highly dependent on any single contributor (there are at least three legally independent committers and there is no single company or entity that is vital to the success of the project),' and podlings over-dependent on one company are told to 'mitigate this requirement by admitting more external contributors' [4]. This is a holistic human judgment made once, at graduation time — not a continuous, retrospectively computed statistic. (Caveat: the Apache Project Maturity Model's CD-series (CD10-CD50), initially expected to hold the diversity criteria, actually covers code-governance/provenance practices, not community diversity [5] — the true diversity-criteria codes in that model were not directly retrieved this session and should be re-verified before being numbered in the paper.) The positioning takeaway: ASF treats authority diffusion as a pass/fail governance checkpoint; this hypothesis proposes a continuous, DOA-computed, retrospective trajectory metric — a different epistemic register, not a duplicate.

(A3) Onboarding/core-formation literature studies the mirror-image process. Jergensen, Sarma & Wagstrom's 'Onion Patch' model describes newcomers migrating from peripheral participation (mailing lists, bug trackers) toward the core (code contribution, design decisions) as skill/reputation accrue [6]. Steinmacher et al.'s systematic literature review (20 primary studies from 291 screened) organizes newcomer barriers into five categories, with prior technical skill, community responsiveness, social-contact centrality, and finding an entry point as most-evidenced [7]. Both study the INWARD trajectory (periphery-to-core); this hypothesis studies the OUTWARD trajectory (founder authority dispersing before departure) — genuinely complementary, not overlapping, and worth citing together in related work.

PART B — Historical, liveness-non-conditioned GitHub data sources.

(B1) GH Archive records 15+ event types [8]. PushEvent's commits array (capped at 20 commits/event) contains only sha, author (name/email), message, a Commits-API url pointer, and a distinct boolean — confirmed to exclude file lists and diffs [9]; force-pushes can even yield an empty commits array while the ref move is still recorded [10]. Two no-token access paths exist: hourly gzipped JSON at data.gharchive.org (direct HTTP) and the public BigQuery `githubarchive` dataset via the free sandbox tier (needs a Google Cloud project, not a GitHub token) [8] — a Snowflake Marketplace listing also exists but is less relevant here. GH Archive event timestamps make it possible to build a 'top-N repos as of year Y' selection frame without reference to present-day liveness, which is exactly the repo-selection capability needed; per-file DOA history is NOT available from GH Archive itself.

(B2) World of Code genuinely holds full commit/blob/file/author cross-reference data at scale (~107M author strings over ~6B commits in its identity-resolution work) [11][12], but the standard access path is an SSH account on the maintainers' servers via a registration form, with account creation same-day to next-day [13] — an approval-dependent process, not instant self-serve. Two narrower self-contained derived artifacts were found (an author-identity map, and a Zenodo-hosted bot-commit-to-project-file-blob mapping covering 150M+ file updates) [14], but neither is a general-purpose no-account full commit-author-file corpus sufficient for DOA computation at the scale this study needs. Verdict: WoC is the right shape of data but access-gated — recommend AGAINST it as the primary path for a short, low-budget execution window; keep as a longer-horizon fallback.

(B3) GHTorrent is confirmed effectively dead: its own shell/DB access reportedly ended July 2019 [15], and a direct fetch of ghtorrent.org/downloads.html in this session returned a 301 redirect to an unrelated third-party (a Chilean school) site, meaning the domain itself has lapsed [16]. A historical GHTorrent-on-BigQuery mirror exists but has not been updated since June 2019 [17] — seven years stale as of 2026. Do not build on GHTorrent. Libraries.io, by contrast, is live: its full dataset is periodically exported as CSV and archived on Zenodo (~5GB compressed / ~25GB uncompressed, CC-BY-SA 4.0, ~25M repositories covered) [18] — usable for the repo-selection frame (no token) but, as expected, carries no commit-level history.

(B4) Recommended concrete pull-path, in priority order: (1) build the repo-selection frame frozen at a chosen historical year Y from GH Archive's hourly dumps or the BigQuery sandbox (or Libraries.io's Zenodo CSV as a cross-check), ranked by stars/forks/watch activity AS OF year Y, mirroring Avelino et al.'s top-N-per-language sampling without conditioning on present-day liveness [8][18]; (2) for each selected repo, obtain full commit/file history via plain unauthenticated `git clone` — unlimited, token-free for public repos, and retrievable regardless of the repo's current activity status; (3) treat World of Code and GHTorrent as non-primary (WoC: credible but access-gated fallback [13]; GHTorrent: dead, do not use [16][17]); (4) report as an honest residual limitation that repos made fully private or permanently deleted (not merely archived/inactive) between year Y and today will still be lost to a `git clone` 404 — a real but much smaller and qualitatively different concern than the present-liveness-conditioning bias this redesign exists to fix.
